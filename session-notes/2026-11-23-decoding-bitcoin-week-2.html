1Why the earlier attempts failed

Bitcoin was not the first try at internet money. The answer to question 1: earlier attempts each solved one piece, but none removed the trusted middleman. DigiCash had strong privacy but ran on one company’s server, so when the company went bankrupt the money died with it. (It worked a lot like Cashu ecash today: deposit money, get digital tokens from an issuer.) b-money and Bit Gold described proof of work and digital scarcity, but stayed on paper.

The missing piece was agreement. Alex put it clearly: if Alice has one coin and sends it to Bob and to Carol at the same time, the network has to agree which payment counts. b-money asked nodes to vote, and the majority wins. The problem: an attacker can spin up a thousand nodes and outvote everyone. Bitcoin’s vote is proof of work. Fake nodes are free; fake work is not.

“All the pieces had been tried before. Satoshi just combined them all together.”

Alex, on standing on the shoulders of giants

2History you can check yourself

Question 2 picked a moment from Bitcoin history. The answer: Satoshi mined the Genesis block, then sent 10 BTC to Hal Finney, the first proof that digital money could move between two people with no one in the middle. Anyone can still verify it with their own node. Then came Pizza Day: 10,000 BTC for two pizzas. keypleb pulled up the transaction on mempool.space: lots of small UTXOs, probably from mining, gathered into one 10,000 BTC output plus a tiny fee.

And then Satoshi left. The last known email, to Mike Hearn: “I’ve moved on to other things. It’s in good hands with Gavin and everyone.” Not a goodbye, just a technical reply. Was it because Gavin was about to present Bitcoin to the CIA? Nobody knows. Alex added the twist: Gavin, Satoshi’s chosen successor, later sided with big blocks in the block size war and lost his leadership role. Even the founder’s pick doesn’t control Bitcoin. The same fights come back (Knots vs Core is the latest), which is why it pays to understand the history.

“In 20 years we’ll look at the mempool and say: this coffee I paid for in Chiang Mai is now worth a car.”

keypleb, on Pizza Day

    3Paint you can’t unmix

    Question 3: what is a hash function? It takes any input, a word, a transaction, a whole block header, and squeezes it into a fixed-size fingerprint. Change one character and the output changes completely. The three properties Bitcoin relies on:

    Three properties, and where they show up

    DeterministicSame in, same outEvery node recomputes transaction IDs and block hashes and gets the same answer. No trust needed.
    One-wayNo way backYou can’t work out the input from the output. That is what makes mining a lottery and hides Lightning secrets.
    Collision resistantNo twinsNobody can find two inputs with the same hash, so every transaction ID is unique.

    keypleb’s aha moment for one-way: buy a can of white paint, pour in some black, mix. You get grey, and you can never unmix it. Razor then showed the Hash Functions tool on Decoding Bitcoin: type any input, get a 64-character output, change one character and every character changes.

    Collisions are not just theory. In 2013 Peter Todd locked bitcoins in scripts that anyone could claim by finding a hash collision. The SHA-1 bounty was claimed in 2017, after Google published the first SHA-1 collision. The SHA-256 bounty is still there. So how likely is a collision? SHA-256 gives 256 bits; thanks to the birthday trick, finding any two matching inputs takes about 2128 tries. Alex’s comparison: it is as hopeless as generating someone else’s seed phrase.

    Then why do some wallets use 24 words? A 12-word seed already has 128 bits of entropy: imagine finding one grain of sand on a 5 km beach (and the real beach is far, far bigger). 24 words gives 256 bits. In practice 12 is enough; 24 is for the extra careful.

    4UTXOs are notes in your wallet

    Question 4 got the clearest metaphor of the night. Bitcoin doesn’t track how much money you have. It tracks the notes you hold. To know your balance, you open your wallet and count. Want to pay 0.3 BTC with a 0.5 BTC note? You can’t cut it in half. You hand over the whole note, the transaction creates two new ones, 0.3 for them and 0.2 back to you as change, and the old note is spent forever.

    One note in, two notes out

    0.5 BTCYour UTXO
    →
    TransactionSpends it whole
    →
    0.3 BTCTo them
    0.2 BTCChange to you

    In a real transaction a small fee comes off the change. The chain stores notes and who can spend them. Your wallet does the adding up.

    Question 5: UTXOs vs accounts. One downside: you can end up with lots of tiny UTXOs (dust), while an account just shows one number. Upsides: UTXOs are independent, so they can be checked in parallel, while Ethereum-style accounts must process each account’s transactions in strict order. And privacy: with a fresh address for every payment, a leak exposes one UTXO, not your whole history in one account.

    Then the best debate of the night: could Bitcoin have worked with accounts? Some said no: UTXOs keep the system closed and sound, nothing enters or leaves except by the rules. Others said yes, with less privacy. Razor’s take: even with accounts, people would create a billion accounts with one coin each, which is “basically a UTXO model with extra steps.” Open homework: if you find a good answer, post it in the chat.

    5Don’t trust, verify

    Question 6 closed the night. A bank asks you to trust it. Bitcoin runs on maths and open code that anyone can check, and nobody can quietly edit. Most Bitcoin losses come from people losing their own keys, not from Bitcoin being hacked. And yes, quantum computers came up: Bitcoin will need to keep improving as that threat grows.

    6The deep end: BOSS and Saving Satoshi

    Alex and Razor have both been through the BOSS Challenge. It runs on challenges at your own pace: Bitcoin Core, building a transaction, Taproot, Lightning and HTLCs, some cryptography. Finish it and they match you with projects, mentors or grant givers. The next round starts in January and the wait list is open.

    “It’s not really a course. It’s more like a recruitment pipeline. But you’re still going to learn a lot.”

    Razor, BOSS Challenge alumni

    To even start BOSS, you need to pass Saving Satoshi, Chaincode’s story-driven coding challenge. Dropout is high and that’s fine: you still learn, meet maintainers and get a feel for the community. Decoding Bitcoin stays the gentle, beginner-friendly start, no coding needed for the questions.

    Key takeaways

    • Bitcoin’s trick is agreement without a referee. Votes can be faked with free nodes. Proof of work can’t.
    • Notes, not balances. Spend whole UTXOs, get change back. Your wallet does the counting.
    • Explaining is learning. Six people taught six questions, and the best moments were the follow-up questions nobody had prepared for.

    Reading

    • Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System, Satoshi Nakamoto, 2008: the introduction names the problem the predecessors could not solve: trust in a central party. Read it with the Bitcoin Reading Club
    • b-money, Wei Dai, 1998: money kept by every participant, with proof-of-work creation but no answer to fake-node votes
    • Bit Gold, Nick Szabo, 2005: chained proof-of-work puzzles as scarce digital gold. Never launched
    • Satoshi's emails, Satoshi Nakamoto Institute: where to find the last email to Mike Hearn in April 2011: "I've moved on to other things"
    • SHA-1 collision bounty, Peter Todd, 2013: bitcoins locked in scripts anyone could claim with a hash collision. The SHA-1 one was claimed in 2017; the SHA-256 one is still there
    • SHAttered, Google and CWI, 2017: the first practical SHA-1 collision. Bitcoin uses SHA-256, which still stands
