Highlights

1One address, nothing to link

The session opened with a real payment. keypleb tapped Request in the Shroud wallet and got an address starting with sp1. keypleb pasted it into Cake Wallet and sent 20,000 sats. Then we looked the transaction up on mempool.space, and there was nothing special to see: two inputs, two outputs, one of them a plain Taproot (v1_p2tr) output. No sp anywhere. That is the trick: you can publish one address forever, and every payment lands somewhere new.

Why that matters. Our guest Sosthène, who builds the Dana wallet, pointed at donation pages that show a plain Bitcoin address. Razor did it live with a well-known privacy project’s page: paste its address into mempool.space and you see every donation since 2020, more than 17 BTC in total, and where the coins went next. Address reuse doesn’t only hurt the receiver. It hurts everyone who pays them, because now their coins are tied to a public address too.

“If you reuse addresses, you’re done. No matter what you do after that, there’s no way to have good privacy.”

Sosthène, Dana wallet

    What silent payments don’t fix. Sosthène was clear: they solve address reuse, the baseline, and nothing more. Collect many donations on separate outputs and then spend them together in one transaction, and you have just told chain analysis they all belong to the same person. Privacy is layered: silent payments, then coin control, PayJoin, CoinJoin, Lightning and ecash on top. And off-chain: cameras off, don’t talk about your stack, maybe skip the Bitcoin t-shirt at the airport. In France this year, home invasions targeting crypto holders made it painfully real.

    2Spot the payment

    keypleb asked the right question: how would chain analysis tell this apart from a PayJoin, which also has two inputs and two outputs? The Taproot output is a hint, not proof. The bigger giveaway was the amount. The payment was exactly 20,000 sats, a number a human typed. The change was 3,026 sats. Analysts also convert every output to dollars or euros at the time of the transaction and look for round numbers there. Sosthène’s tip: when you ask to be paid, add a few random sats.

    3Scanning is the hard part

    Sending a silent payment is easy: no full node, just follow the protocol. Receiving is the work. The receiver’s wallet has to check every transaction to see if one is for them, and different wallets do that differently. That is why some wallets see your payment while it is still in the mempool and others only after it is mined.

    • Full node. Scan the whole chain and the mempool yourself. Best privacy, most effort.
    • Electrum-style server (Cake Wallet, and Frigate behind Sparrow). Fast, sees mempool payments, but the server learns which outputs are yours, so you trust it.
    • BlindBit Oracle (Dana). Serves the same public data to everyone, so it learns little more than your IP and that you use silent payments. Today it can’t see mempool payments: you wait for a block.

    Along the way Sosthène explained the gap limit: a normal wallet only checks so many unused addresses ahead. keypleb told the story of a client whose two wallets showed different balances from the same seed. Two days of troubleshooting later, raising the gap limit made the “missing” bitcoin appear. Silent payments don’t have that problem, but they trade it for scanning.

    4Razor builds it from scratch

    Razor started with history. Silent payments are BIP 352, by Ruben Somsen and Josie Baker. Before them came stealth addresses, BIP 47 reusable payment codes (Samourai), which need a notification transaction, and BIP 351, which uses OP_RETURN. Then his favourite slide, the interactivity scale:

    The interactivity scale

    How much do sender and receiver have to talk before a payment?

    Silent paymentsPost one address. Everyone just pays it. No back and forth.
    A fresh address per payerAn HD wallet or BTCPay Server makes a new address for each person you give it to.
    PayJoinNew address, a PSBT sent back and forth, a relay and a mailbox directory.
    Least interactiveMost interactive

    How a silent payment works

    Shared secret from the sender’s input key and the receiver’s address. Only the receiver can redo it.

    SenderSpends a coin as usual. Its input already reveals a public key B, and only the sender knows b.
    →
    Shared secretSender computes b·A from the sp1 address. Receiver computes a·B. Same number (Diffie-Hellman).
    →
    Fresh Taproot outputThe secret tweaks the receiver’s key into a brand new output. It looks like any other Taproot payment.
    tweak t = hash( shared secret )
    output key = A + t·G   →  spend with a + t
    receiver scans: for every transaction, a·(input keys) → does any output match?

    The real BIP adds more: what happens with many inputs, and a counter so two payments from the same key still give different outputs. If you follow the five steps above, the BIP reads easily. Razor’s tip: start at silentpayments.xyz.

    5Where you can help

    Sosthène dropped the projects in the chat, and was blunt: they are built by very few people and could definitely use help. Dana (donations wallet), BlindBit Oracle (privacy-friendly scanning server), Frigate (silent payments Electrum server behind Sparrow) and Shroud with its indexer. Razor’s way in: build and run the project, break the tests, do reviews. Want a recommendation to a grant giver later? Show proof of work first.

    Key takeaways

    • Never reuse an address. It leaks your history and everyone else’s. A silent payment address is the one you can safely post on a website.
    • One layer, not the whole stack. Silent payments fix the baseline. Don’t merge those coins carelessly, avoid round amounts, and add PayJoin and coin control.
    • Scanning is the frontier. How a wallet finds its payments decides speed and privacy, and it is exactly where the projects need developers.
